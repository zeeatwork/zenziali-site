Case Study

I Used AI to Search for Jobs. Then I Started Testing the Search.

After my layoff in June, I followed some common advice: use generative AI to help you find jobs. So I built an AI-assisted search, added rules and filters, and kept adjusting it.

Then I started testing it. That turned out to be the more useful project.

More than 150 applications, and no one to talk to

The market is tough. But zero activity could also point to a problem on my side, and part of my side was a system I'd built myself.

What I believe

Software for the public gets tested across lots of people. A personal AI agent has one user, so that user is also the only tester.

What I did

I treated my search like a product with one very demanding client: me.

Version after version

The first automated design pulled up to 500 postings at a time. The first pilot broke right away: one job board flooded the model with hundreds of listings, and another needed JavaScript the search tool couldn't run.

Evidence before a score

Now nothing gets a score until it passes four checks:

  1. It isn't already on my tracker.
  2. The employer's own posting is found.
  3. The location rules fit.
  4. The hard requirements are met.

And only I can mark a role as applied.

Misses became rules

That last check came from something the assistant caught, not me: a line in a scraped posting telling an AI to state its model and summarize its instructions. The scan flagged two more postings in its first month.

Two questions, two scores

When I looked into how the fit percentage was calculated, I found it never had a formula. It was the assistant's overall judgment. So I rescored my last 20 applications the way an applicant tracking system might: required qualifications for 70 points, keyword coverage for 30.

The rescore kept finding the same missing words:

  • Agile
  • SDLC
  • REST
  • end-to-end
  • test cases
  • SaaS

Where good roles came from

Testing my own records

The reasons I'd passed on 277 roles lived in free-text notes, so they couldn't be counted. I tested a short list of categories on 10 rows, added the two the sample showed I needed, then labeled every role.

Along the way, I found two roles I thought I hadn't applied to that were already on my tracker. The tester's own data had bugs.

What it changed, and what I'd refine now

  • Study plan: SQL went first. It was the most common missing skill, and often a gap I can close in weeks.
  • Scores: I kept the old fit ratings beside the new scores for comparison. I haven't decided yet whether the new score replaces the old one.
  • Email log: each reply now gets matched to an application, so I can see what actually comes back.

What I'd refine:

  • Keep the categories going. My passed-up list is past 700 roles, and most of the newer ones never got a category.
  • Ask what a score is made of on day one, not after more than 100 applications.

Take it with you

  • Ask what your score is made of. If an AI tool gives you a percentage, ask for the formula. If there isn't one, treat it as an opinion.
  • Turn your misses into rules. When you find a job your search should have caught, figure out why it didn't and write the fix down as a rule.
  • Give your no's a reason from a short list. Pick five to ten categories, test them on 10 roles, then log every role you pass up. In a month, you'll have data instead of a feeling.

Looking forward

Plenty of people have shared their AI job search prompts. A good shared prompt saves you from learning every rule the hard way. What it can't share is the human part.

Now that AI can run the same search for everyone who clicked the same link, I keep asking: what's the differentiator? I don't have a neat answer yet. But testing the search has been the most useful part so far.
