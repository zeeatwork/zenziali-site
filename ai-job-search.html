Case Study

I Used AI to Search for Jobs. Then I Started Testing the Search.

After my layoff in June, I followed some common advice: use generative AI to help you find jobs. So I built an AI-assisted search, added rules and filters, and kept adjusting it.

Then I started testing it. That turned out to be the more useful project.

More than 150 applications, and no one to talk to

By Oct 20, my tracker had more than 150 applications dated since June. Not one had led to a phone screen, a personal reply, or a rejection that wasn't a template. A few rejections arrived within hours, which looked a lot like an automated filter.

The market is tough, and I know that. But zero activity could also point to a problem on my side. And part of my side was a system I'd built myself.

What I believe

Lots of people are building their own AI job search agents right now. I understand why. You get a tool with context about your skills and the kind of place you want to work, and it gets sharper the more you tell it.

But software made for the general public is tested to work across a lot of people. A personal agent is built for one person. That person is the only user, so they're also the only tester. If nobody tests it, nobody finds out where it's wrong.

What I did

I treated my search like a product with one very demanding client: me. It went through a lot of versions. A daily alert. Then a manual search. Then a more automated search, with some web crawling and some manual testing. And so on.

The first design was built for volume: pull up to 500 recent postings, remove duplicates, score them, and send the high scorers to a To Apply list. The first pilot hit real limits right away. One large job board flooded the model with hundreds of listings, and another needed JavaScript the search tool couldn't run.

So the search moved from volume to evidence. Before anything gets a score, it checks my tracker for duplicates, finds the employer's own posting, checks the location rules, and checks hard requirements. And I keep submission control: the assistant can't mark anything applied without my confirmation.

Some of the best test cases came from jobs I found myself. In October, I shared a role at a healthcare software company that the search had never surfaced. It had only seen a different role at the same company, on a job board, and it couldn't read the employer's own site, so it set that one aside as needing a manual check. Once I verified my role, it rated 91%. On Oct 19, the rules changed: a posting the search can't verify now gets a provisional rating, and any at 85% or higher goes to the top of my daily report so I can find the employer's link myself.

Another miss was a rule doing exactly what it was told. The search found a role at a consumer reviews company and declined it over a degree requirement. When I checked the employer's listing, the degree was only preferred. I restored the role and applied. The next day, the rule changed: required qualifications are hard stops, a degree passes when the posting accepts equivalent experience, and preferred items only lower the rating.

Misses went the other way, too. In late October, one feed let through two city-based roles that weren't remote. I caught them, and the fix was a check on the posting's remote wording.

Each mistake became a rule, the way a bug becomes a regression test.

That last rule came from something the assistant caught, not me: a line in a scraped posting that told an AI to state its model and summarize its instructions. After I made scanning for it a rule, the scan flagged two more postings in its first month.

Then I tested the score itself. When I looked into how the fit percentage was calculated, I found it never had a formula. It was the assistant's overall judgment, with no weights. So I rescored my last 20 applications with a method closer to how applicant tracking systems sort candidates.

The gaps repeated. My resume never said Agile or SDLC, and literal terms like REST, end-to-end, test cases, and SaaS were often missing.

I measured where good roles actually came from, too. Between Sep 30 and Oct 23, two job boards produced about 12 of the 13 roles strong enough to tailor materials for. Searches of employers' own hiring sites produced none of about 44 candidates, and my company directory produced none of about 34. So I changed where the search looks first.

I tested my own records, too. I'd logged 277 roles I passed up, but the reasons lived in free-text notes, so they couldn't be counted. I tried a short list of categories on 10 rows first, added the two the sample showed I needed, and then gave every role a primary and secondary reason. Along the way, I found two roles I thought I hadn't applied to that were already on my tracker. The tester's own data had bugs.

What it changed, and what I'd refine now

I didn't throw out the old score. I rescored those 20 applications the new way and kept the old ratings beside them for comparison. I haven't decided yet whether the new score replaces the old one in the daily search. I also started an email log that matches each reply to an application, so I can see what actually comes back.

The passed-up list shaped my study plan. SQL came up more than any other missing skill, and it's often a small gap I can close in weeks, so I put it first.

What I'd refine: the categories only help if I keep using them. My passed-up list has grown past 700 roles, and most of the newer ones never got a category. I'd also ask what a score is made of on day one, not after more than 100 applications.

Take it with you

  • Ask what your score is made of. If an AI tool gives you a percentage, ask for the formula. If there isn't one, treat it as an opinion.
  • Turn your misses into rules. When you find a job your search should have caught, figure out why it didn't and write the fix down as a rule.
  • Give your no's a reason from a short list. Pick five to ten categories, test them on 10 roles, then log every role you pass up. In a month, you'll have data instead of a feeling.

Looking forward

I thought about sharing my setup, but plenty of people have already shared their AI job search prompts. A good shared prompt saves you from learning every rule the hard way. What it can't share is the human part.

Now that AI can run the same search for everyone who clicked the same link, I keep coming back to one question: what's the differentiator? And how do I use and build a tool like this so it adds to my career, not just my application count? I don't have a neat answer yet. But testing the search has been the most useful part of it so far.
