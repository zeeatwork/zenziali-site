About Me

I came to quality through curiosity.

My path into Quality Engineering wasn't a straight line. Before I tested software, I worked in roles where understanding people, organizing complicated work, spotting patterns, and improving processes were already part of the job. When I moved into technology, quality felt like a natural extension of those skills.

Over the past five years, I've worked across software testing, quality strategy, test automation, accessibility, and release processes. I've also learned that some of my most useful contributions happen before a test is ever executed: asking questions, identifying risk, connecting information, and helping teams build better ways of working.

Building the Foundation

My technical foundation has grown alongside my work in quality. Early in my career, I could often recognize patterns and find my way toward a solution before I fully understood everything happening underneath it. Experience gave me more context. Working with browser automation, APIs, accessibility, CI/CD, test data, and different application architectures gradually connected concepts that once felt abstract to the problems I was solving every day.

That process is still important to me. I deliberately return to fundamentals, build things, experiment with unfamiliar tools, and strengthen the parts of my technical knowledge that I want to understand more deeply. The goal isn't to collect technologies. It's to become better at reasoning about software, investigating failures, and deciding what evidence a team needs to trust what it's building.

How I Think About Quality

I don't see Quality Engineering as simply finding defects before customers do. I see it as helping a team understand risk. Sometimes that means executing tests or building automation. Sometimes it means asking a question that changes a requirement, improving the information captured in a bug report, noticing a gap in a release process, or recognizing a pattern before it becomes a larger problem.

That is also why I care about the systems surrounding testing. The testing I do should leave a team with more than a list of passed and failed tests. It should create useful information, make decisions easier, and help the next cycle of work go better than the last one.

I value collaboration because quality rarely belongs to one person or one role. The best testing I've done has come from understanding what developers are building, what stakeholders need, how customers actually use the product, and where those perspectives don't quite line up.

Still Developing

I think of professional growth as an ongoing part of the work. Right now, I'm deepening my JavaScript skills, building automation projects, and creating realistic Quality Engineering exercises that let me practice technical problem-solving in the context where I actually use it.

I'm also learning how AI changes the way we work, and evaluating AI model outputs has made that learning hands-on. I want enough practical knowledge of the processes and technologies I use to direct AI effectively, recognize when an output isn't good enough, and understand when doing something myself is the better choice. For me, learning and using AI aren't competing goals. Stronger fundamentals make the tools more useful.

I'm interested in what comes next, whether that means deeper work in Quality Engineering, test automation and infrastructure, or opportunities that sit somewhere between quality, software development, and AI. What matters most to me is continuing to solve interesting problems, understand the systems behind them, and leave the work better than I found it.
